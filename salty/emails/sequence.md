# The follow-up sequence

Twelve emails: five welcomes (one per sign-up type, sent within the hour) and seven follow-ups shared by everyone. Read `README.md` first for who gets which and how to load them.

Placeholders to replace or map in your email tool:

- `[site]`: the live address of Salty.
- `[plan link]`: where the full raise plan is sold. Until it is on sale, link to `[site]` and change "Get the full plan" to "Build your free plan".
- `[job]`: the `job_title` field, falling back to "your job" when it is empty.
- `[Your name]`: you. These are written in the first person on purpose.

Each email has a UK and a US line where an example figure is used; send the one matching the `country` field, and the UK one to everyone else.

### Welcome C: checked a job offer (`offer`)

**Subject:** Before you reply to that offer
**Preview:** Three rules, two minutes.

Hi,

You checked a job offer on Salty. Before you answer it, three rules:

1. **Never answer on the spot.** "Thank you, I'm really pleased. Can I come back to you tomorrow?" is always acceptable.
2. **Counter once, in writing, with a number.** Not "is there any flexibility?" but "I was expecting something closer to X. Is there room to move?" One polite ask is a normal part of being hired.
3. **Look at the whole package.** Hours, holiday, pension and the date of your first pay review can be worth more than the difference in salary, and they are easier for an employer to give.

## 2. Three days later

**Subject:** The mistake that costs most people their raise
**Preview:** It is about when, not what.

Hi,

The most common mistake in asking for a raise has nothing to do with what you say. It is when you say it.

Most people wait for their pay review. By then the budget for the year has often been decided, and your manager is telling you a number, not discussing one.

Ask two or three weeks before the review. If no review is coming, do not wait for one: ask for twenty minutes this week, ideally just after something has gone well.

If you have not looked at where your pay sits yet, start there. It gives you the figure the whole conversation rests on: [site]

## 3. One week later

**Subject:** What to say when they say "there's no budget"
**Preview:** It is the most common answer. Here is the reply.

Hi,

"There's no budget" is the answer people fear most, and it is the easiest one to handle, because it is about timing and not about you.

Say this:

> I understand. Can we agree the figure now and the date it starts? If it can't be this quarter, I'd like the date in writing.

You have just turned a no into a yes with a date on it. If they will not agree a date either, you have learned something more useful than the raise: that the answer here is no.

## 5. Two months later

**Subject:** What "I'll ask next year" actually costs
**Preview:** A sum worth doing once.

Hi,

Here is a sum worth doing once.

Take the gap between your pay and the middle for your job. Divide it by twelve. That is what waiting costs you each month.

If the gap is £3,000 a year ($3,000 in the US), that is £250 a month. Wait a year to ask and you have given away the whole £3,000.

It also grows. Most future raises are a percentage of what you earn now, so a lower figure today means a smaller raise every year after.

None of this means you will get the full gap by asking. It means asking has a price and so does not asking, and only one of them is ever discussed.
